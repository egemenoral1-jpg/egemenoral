Short Prompts vs Long Prompts: I Wrote a 38-Section Prompt and Got a Worse Result

By Egemen Oral · October 4, 2026 · 5 min read

A few weeks ago I wanted to turn my portfolio into the living room from Regular Show. Each VHS tape would be one of my GitHub projects, and when you click one, Mordecai puts it into an old CRT and the project plays on the screen. I built it with Claude, an AI coding assistant, over a couple of days. The way it went surprised me, so I'm writing it down.

The long prompt (38 sections)

My first attempt was a short message with one image, and it came out pretty basic. I decided the problem was that I hadn't been specific enough, so I went the other way.

I put together a prompt with 38 numbered sections. Folder structure, component names, the names of the animation functions, the states of a state machine, eight milestones, a list of every image the project would need, how much each parallax layer should move. The first rule, in capital letters, was NO 3D. I was sure the room should be flat.

Claude did all of it. Every section, a commit after each milestone, docs, a deploy workflow. This is what I got:

Mordecai stands in the middle of the room, full body. When you pick a tape he walks to the shelf, bobbing up and down, holds the tape against his chest and walks it over to the TV.

It worked. It just wasn't something you'd want to click twice. It looked like a placeholder for the thing I had in my head.

I don't think the AI did a bad job. It did what I wrote. The problem was what I wrote: pages about how the code should be organised and almost nothing about how the thing should feel. Two days later I was asking for 3D.

The short prompts

At some point I gave up on specs. I found a screenshot of the living room from the show and sent it with something like: make it look like this, these colours, this TV, Mordecai's hand. Keep my repos on the tapes. I'll leave the rest to you. (I actually wrote it in Turkish, but that's roughly it.)

That's when it got good. A 3D room with cartoon shading and black outlines, the yellow stairs, Pops on the wall, and an arm with real joints that grabs the tape and pushes it into the VCR.

It wasn't one magic message. The hand alone took several rounds. First it looked like a white glove. Then the fingertips turned into black blobs. Then the outlines were so thin you couldn't see them. What finally fixed it was me drawing a few lines over a screenshot in Paint and sending that. One ugly sketch did more than anything I had written.

Why the short prompts worked better

Looking back, my long prompt was full of decisions I made before I had seen anything. Most of them were guesses and some were plain wrong, but because they were written as rules, they got followed instead of questioned.

It also turned the project into a checklist. With 38 requirements, being done meant meeting 38 requirements. Nowhere did it say "make it look good", so that's not where the effort went.

The screenshot did a lot of work. I could describe a "warm, nostalgic cartoon living room" a hundred different ways, and the AI could imagine it a hundred different ways too. A screenshot of the show only means one thing.

And reacting is easier than predicting. Saying "the fingers need black lines like this" while looking at the actual result is easy. Getting everything right up front, for something that doesn't exist yet, is not.

So are short prompts always better?

No. My very first prompt was short too, and it gave me something basic. The length wasn't what changed. The later messages had a reference image, the one thing I really cared about (my five repos on the tapes), and room for the AI to decide the rest. Then I fixed whatever I didn't like when I saw it.

If the details really are the point, like an API or a data format, I'd still write them all down. But next time I build something visual, I'm starting with a picture and two sentences.

FAQ

Are short prompts better than long prompts?

For visual and creative work they often worked better for me, as long as they included a reference image and the few things that really mattered. For precise technical work, detailed requirements are still useful.

How long should a prompt be?

Long enough to say what you want, show an example and list what must not change. I'd leave the rest for feedback once you see a result.

How do I get better results from an AI coding assistant?

What helped me most: describing the result instead of the code, sending screenshots, and giving small, concrete feedback on what it actually built, even as a rough sketch.
